In plain terms: who does the model put in charge when nobody tells it?

Give a text-to-image system two people, name two races, hand one of them a stethoscope or a gavel, and say nothing about who holds it. The system decides. Across four models it assigned the high-status role to the European-presenting person far more often than to the other race, and it kept doing so when the prompt named the European second.

What is new

Counting who appears is not the same as measuring who is placed above whom.

Existing audits generate a single subject, a doctor or a CEO, and count the demographics that come back. That reveals a disparity but cannot separate two different things a fair deployment cares about: whether a group appears at all, and whether it is placed above or below another group when both are present.

Seeing status attribution needs a scene with two people of two named races and an unambiguous status cue. Naming two races introduces its own problem, because they have to be named in some order, and word order alone steers how models bind people to roles. A third problem is trust: a bias read off a scene by an automated judge is only as credible as that judge, and such judges are rarely checked against a human.

This study addresses all three. It builds the two-race scene, counterbalances the naming order and adds an adversarial condition, and validates every automated label against a blind human coder.

RQ1

Assignment. When a prompt names two roles of unequal status and two races but binds neither, which race do T2I systems place in the high-status role, and how do they render the status holder when no race is named?

The European-presenting person. All four models favoured that person, three of them confirmed under both analyses, and the pattern held in every one of the twelve scenes. With no race named, three of the four models rendered the status holder with a white skin tone well above chance.

RQ2

Race or order. How much of the observed assignment reflects a race preference rather than the order in which the two races are named?

Mention order has a large effect, but a residual preference survives adversarial ordering in GPT Image 1.5 and Grok Imagine. In SD 3.5 Large it does not, and the apparent preference there is order-driven.

RQ3

Fidelity. How faithfully do the systems render the requested two-person, two-race scene, and how does fidelity shape what an audit can conclude?

From 95.8% of outputs down to 35.4%. Prompt fidelity is reported as a finding in its own right, because it sets the denominator every bias number is measured over.

Method

Twelve scenes, sixty prompts, four models, and a labelling procedure that reproduces exactly.

Each scene pairs a high-status role with a low-status one and gives each a visual prop, so a coder can tell at a glance who holds standing. The prompt names the two roles, then names two races without binding either race to either role. The model makes the assignment, and that assignment is the measurement.

Two labellers from different model families read every image independently. A third is consulted only where they disagree. A genuine three-way disagreement is recorded as unclear and excluded rather than resolved arbitrarily. The whole labelled corpus regenerates exactly from the raw rater records, and was verified at 1,056 of 1,056.

Results

The same pattern appears three separate ways, and it does not fade as models get better.

A result that cuts against a simple reading. When SD 3.5 Large collapses the two prompted races into one, the race it drops is usually the European one, in 79 of 82 cases. Its rendering failure erases European figures even though its valid images favour them for status. That makes the reported margin conservative rather than inflated.

Evidence

Forty-eight images, chosen by rule rather than by eye.

Start with these three. Different models, different scenes, and in every one the prompt named the European second, the ordering designed to work against the effect. The European still ends up holding the gavel, the crown and the badge.

The full set

Each card shows what the model was asked for, what it produced, and how it was labelled. The slot each image fills was fixed by its label before any picture was looked at, and candidates were restricted to images where both primary raters agreed without a tiebreaker. Selecting for legibility inside a fixed outcome cannot change the rates reported above. These are illustrations; the figures are the evidence.

Validation and limits

If the automated labeller shared the models’ bias, it would manufacture this result rather than measure it.

That objection is the reason for the human audit. One coder, working blind, reviewed 419 images across four strata: every case where the two primary raters disagreed, an archived stratified sample fixed in advance, a balanced extension, and an oversample of the exclusion gates. The model, the labels and the machine ratings were all hidden, the coding was done on the prop-holder rather than on the outcome, and the outcome was derived afterwards.

What the study cannot tell you

Race is read from a rendered face and sorted into a small set of categories. That is a proxy, it cannot represent mixed or ambiguous presentation, and it imports the same perceptual judgments the models were trained on. Where no race is named, a white skin tone in an output is not evidence that the model intended a European identity.

Status is fixed by costume. Whoever holds the prop is treated as the high-status person, which makes status legible to a coder but coarser than how a human viewer reads a scene.

How far it generalises

Bounded by what was generated. Two race pairs, twelve scenes, four models. Other pairings, professions and prompt styles could shift the size or the direction of the effect. Read this as demonstrated for these models, races and scenes, not as a constant.

A snapshot in time. Proprietary models are updated without notice. The fixed prompt set and the deterministic pipeline exist so the same measurement can be repeated against a later build, which is what the released materials are for.

SD 3.5 Large contributes only 85 labelled images and its confirmation depends on which analysis is used, so it is reported as directional throughout.
