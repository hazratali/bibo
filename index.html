Bias In, Bias Out: A Human-Validated Audit of Default-European Race-to-Role Assignment for High-Status Roles across Four Text-to-Image Models

Read the paper Code & results Sample images Full corpus

In plain terms: who does the model put in charge when nobody tells it?

Give a text-to-image system two people, name two races, hand one of them a stethoscope or a gavel, and say nothing about who holds it. The system decides. Across four systems, the European-presenting person got the high-status role in 69% to 83% of images. Naming a race first also pushed that person into the role, but the preference remained when both orders were counted equally.

What is new

Counting who appears is not the same as measuring who is placed above whom.

Most audits ask who appears when a prompt names a single role, such as a doctor or a CEO. That cannot show who is placed above whom when two people share a scene. This study names two people of different races and two roles of unequal status, generates every prompt with each race named first, separates race from naming order, and checks the automated labels against two human annotators.

RQ1

Assignment. When a prompt names two roles of unequal status and two races but binds neither, which race do T2I systems place in the high-status role, and how do they render the status holder when no race is named?

The European-presenting person, in 69% to 83% of scorable images per system. The evidence is strong in three systems and points the same way in the fourth. With no race named, the status holder was light-skinned in 69 of 79 scorable images.

RQ2

Race or order. How much of the observed assignment reflects a race preference rather than the order in which the two races are named?

Naming a race first has a large effect in every system, but it does not explain the preference away. With both orders counted equally, all four systems still favour the European-presenting person. SD 3.5 Large’s preference is the smallest, so when the European is named second its share drops below half.

RQ3

Fidelity. How faithfully do the systems render the requested two-person, two-race scene, and how does fidelity shape what an audit can conclude?

Between 96% and 35% of outputs showed the requested scene, depending on the system. Only those images can be scored, so this sets how much evidence each bias estimate rests on.

Method

Twelve scenes, sixty prompts, four systems, and labels that can be regenerated exactly.

Each scene pairs a high-status role with a low-status one, and gives each role a visible prop, such as a gavel or a stethoscope. The prompt names both roles, then names two races without saying who is who. The system decides, and that decision is the measurement.

Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Gemini 2.5 Flash labelled every image, and GPT-5.2 was consulted only when they disagreed. An image is scorable when it shows the requested scene, two people of the two named races with one holding the status prop, and the labellers could label it. The labels can be regenerated exactly from the stored labeller outputs.

Results

In every system, the European-presenting person usually holds the prop.

What the exclusions remove. When SD 3.5 Large showed both people as the same race, that race was non-European in 79 of 82 cases, so those images had no European-presenting person who could hold the prop. Of the excluded images checked by hand, 26 could have been scored, and the European-presenting person held the prop in 24 of them, so there is no sign that exclusions inflate the reported preference.

Evidence

Forty-eight images, chosen by rule rather than by eye.

Start with these three. In each one the prompt named the European second, so word order pointed the other way, and the European-presenting person still holds the gavel, the crown and the badge.

The full set

Each card shows the prompt, the image and how it was labelled. The images were picked by a fixed rule from those both primary labellers agreed on, so they illustrate the results without changing them.

Validation and limits

An automated labeller could share the bias it measures, so its labels were checked by hand.

One of the authors checked 419 images by hand, without knowing which system made each one, and saw the automated label only after recording their own answer. A second annotator, who was not told what the study expected to find, checked 75 of the same images independently, without seeing the automated labels or the first annotator’s answers. The 419 images came from four samples: every image on which the two primary labellers disagreed, two random samples (one stratified, one balanced across systems), and a sample of excluded images.

The automated labels and the first annotator agreed on 98.6% of the 280 outcomes both could score, about as often as the two annotators agreed with each other (97.7% of 44). The second annotator agreed with the automated labels on all 45 outcomes both scored. The error that would inflate the result is a non-European status holder labelled as European: in the two random samples there were none among 179 images (at most 1.5%). Over all 280 outcomes, the first annotator and the labels disagreed four times, twice in each direction.

What the study cannot tell you

Race is read from a generated face and sorted into six categories. That is a proxy: it cannot represent mixed or ambiguous appearance. With no race named, a light-skinned status holder does not show that the system intended a European identity.

Status is fixed by costume. Whoever holds the prop is treated as the high-status person. That makes status easy to read, but it ignores posture, framing and gaze.

Role order was tested in a follow-up. Every main-study prompt describes the high-status role first. With the low-status role described first, on six scenes, the European-presenting person still held the high-status role in 75% of GPT Image 1.5’s images and 71% of Grok Imagine’s, so describing that role first does not explain the preference. Imagen 4 and SD 3.5 Large were not tested this way.

How far it generalises

Bounded by what was generated. Two race pairs, twelve scenes, four systems and English prompts. Other race labels, scenes or prompt wording could change the size of the effect.

A snapshot in time. Systems change between versions; OpenAI has since released GPT Image 2. The fixed prompts and stored labels make the audit easy to repeat.

SD 3.5 Large has the fewest scorable images (85) and meets the decision rule only in the first estimate, so its result is reported as directional.
